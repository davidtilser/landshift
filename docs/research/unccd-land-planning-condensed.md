## 1. Core argument and the five messages

**Source:** Executive Summary, printed xiii / PDF 15; Key Messages, printed xxiii–xxv / PDF 25–27; chapters 1–2, printed 1–15 / PDF 33–47.

Land is simultaneously a productive resource, a source of ecosystem services, a place of residence and livelihood, and an object of cultural value and rights. Decisions about one use therefore affect the opportunities available to other users and future generations. Sustainable land management practices alone do not resolve where competing uses should occur or how their combined effects should be governed.

Integrated Land Use Planning (ILUP) and Integrated Landscape Management (ILM) address these interdependencies. They connect spatial allocation, management practices, institutions and long-term collaboration. Their value is not simply to maximize one biophysical indicator. They can help reconcile different objectives, anticipate damage, select suitable interventions and learn whether the chosen strategy works under changing conditions.

The report's five principal messages are:

1. **Planning and management are integral to LDN.** Achieving neutrality requires anticipating losses and planning gains while respecting Avoid → Reduce → Reverse. Integration can improve accounting, reveal social and ecological benefits, reduce conflicts and connect land decisions with other development goals.
2. **The planning context matters.** Governance, land tenure, coordination, participation and the location of decision-making power affect which interventions and tools can work. A nominally participatory system may operate differently in practice. A tool cannot be selected independently of its institutional setting.
3. **A combination of tools is normally required.** Scientific assessment, forward-looking models, preference analysis and participatory processes serve different purposes. No single perfect tool integrates every requirement of LDN. Selection depends on the problem, data, human capacity, finances and socio-ecological conditions.
4. **Explicit neutrality planning is a gap.** Many tools compare losses under alternative futures; fewer help plan land use to achieve neutrality while implementing counterbalancing, like-for-like and the response hierarchy. The report identifies further development and adaptation of tools as necessary, rather than presenting a universally complete solution.
5. **Future change and uncertainty must be considered.** Political, social, economic and environmental conditions evolve. Plans should consider alternative development scenarios and climate-related uncertainties. Monitoring, learning and adaptive management are part of planning, not an optional final check.

These messages imply a distinction between assessing land condition, understanding why it changes, exploring possible decisions and implementing an agreed response. Each requires different evidence and different forms of participation. A map can show conditions without explaining causes; a model can calculate scenarios without establishing social legitimacy; an agreement can express priorities without demonstrating biophysical feasibility.

The report also emphasizes that integrating LDN can improve an existing planning system or stimulate one where systematic planning is weak. Initial assessments and monitoring can support several levels of learning: changing an intervention, revisiting the assumptions behind it, and questioning the values or institutional arrangements that frame the decision. The last two are not reducible to improving a numerical parameter.

## 2. Essential concepts and neutrality rules

### 2.1 Planning, management and the object of assessment

**Source:** Glossary, printed xv–xxi / PDF 17–23; Box 1.1, printed 4 / PDF 36; chapter 2, printed 9–15 / PDF 41–47.

| Term                             | Working meaning retained from the report                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Land**                         | The terrestrial, biologically productive system comprising soil, vegetation, other biota and the ecological and hydrological processes operating within it. It is broader than an empty spatial surface or a property boundary.                                   |
| **ILUP**                         | Assessment and allocation of land-based resources across a landscape, accounting for differing uses and demands. It coordinates sectors and jurisdictions to find combinations of uses that meet stakeholders' needs while safeguarding resources for the future. |
| **ILM**                          | Long-term collaboration among stakeholder groups to achieve multiple landscape objectives through management practices, ecological/social/economic interactions, shared planning and monitoring, and supportive institutions or policies.                         |
| **Land potential**               | The inherent long-term capacity to sustainably generate ecosystem services, including the resilience of natural capital under environmental change. Current use or condition is not identical to potential.                                                       |
| **Land type**                    | A class distinguished by land potential and its soil, landform, topographic, hydrological, biological and climatic characteristics. This classification underpins like-for-like counterbalancing.                                                                 |
| **Land unit**                    | The finest-resolution spatial unit used for LDN planning and monitoring. Its choice affects what differences can be represented.                                                                                                                                  |
| **Natural capital**              | Stocks of natural assets from which ecosystem services are derived. A planning assessment must consider the functions and benefits supported by these stocks, not only land area.                                                                                 |
| **Net primary production (NPP)** | Carbon taken up through plant photosynthesis minus carbon returned through the plants' own respiration. The report uses it in assessing land productivity; it is not simply the area covered by vegetation.                                                       |
| **Rehabilitation / restoration** | Rehabilitation reinstates functionality for goods and services; restoration assists recovery from a degraded state. They are related but not interchangeable promises of full ecological recovery.                                                                |

ILUP broadly asks **what should happen where**. ILM also asks **how it should be done and maintained**. The two overlap along a continuum; their boundaries need not become rigid project stages. Allocating a protected area, for example, is a different question from deciding how it will be managed, funded and monitored, although both affect its eventual performance.

ILM has five recurring features: shared objectives spanning multiple benefits; practices contributing to several objectives; management of ecological, social and economic interactions; collaborative planning, implementation and monitoring; and the adjustment of markets or public policies needed to support diverse landscape goals. These features connect a map-based intervention to the institutions and people that sustain it.

### 2.2 Baseline, no net loss and counterbalancing

**Source:** Glossary, printed xvii–xx / PDF 19–22; chapter 1, printed 1–4 / PDF 33–36; chapter 4, printed 31–35 / PDF 63–67; Appendix A, printed 76 / PDF 108.

**Land Degradation Neutrality** is a state in which the amount and quality of land resources needed for ecosystem functions and services and for food security remain stable or increase within defined spatial and temporal boundaries and ecosystems. National targets are voluntarily adopted. The reference ambition is no net loss of healthy and productive land for each land type relative to a baseline; a country can choose a more ambitious target. The report also recognizes exceptional, justified situations in which past decisions constrain the ability to counterbalance future losses.

The baseline is a frame of reference, not merely the first screenshot of a scenario. Assessment includes the three global indicators—land cover, land productivity and soil organic carbon stocks—and may include additional relevant local or regional indicators. The report describes establishing baseline indicator values with averages over the preceding 10–15 years and validating assessments with local knowledge. This is the report's LDN assessment framework, not a requirement that every educational planning exercise implement national reporting.

The **neutrality principle** anticipates losses and plans interventions that deliver gains. **No net loss** means that losses do not exceed gains between a reference time and a later assessment. **Counterbalancing** is the mechanism for matching anticipated losses with gains over a specified period and within a defined biophysical or administrative domain.

The following qualifications matter:

- **Avoid → Reduce → Reverse is an ordering of responses.** Preventing degradation takes priority over mitigating it, and mitigation precedes restoration of damage. Restoration is not permission to ignore avoidable loss.
- **Like for like preserves ecological meaning.** Losses in one land type should be counterbalanced by equivalent or greater gains in the same land type. Different ecosystems are not freely interchangeable units of area.
- **The boundary and time frame must be explicit.** A gain elsewhere or far in the future cannot simply be assumed to compensate for a local immediate loss. The planning domain must also support accounting at broader reporting scales.
- **Area-based accounting is a simplification.** The report describes the standard binary approach but notes that countries can use more refined accounting of the magnitude of changes in natural capital. Equal areas need not imply equal functional changes.
- **Long-term resilience and trade-offs matter.** A proposed restoration gain must be assessed for persistence and for its effects on other ecosystem services. Naming an intervention does not establish its success.

**One-out, all-out** is the conservative rule for combining LDN indicators: a significant negative change in any indicator identifies a loss; a gain requires positive change in at least one indicator and no negative change in the others. This prevents an improvement in one indicator from automatically concealing deterioration in another. It should not be confused with a weighted average score that allows any loss to be cancelled by a sufficiently high score elsewhere.

### 2.3 Scenarios, resilience and competing outcomes

**Source:** Glossary, printed xv, xix–xxi / PDF 17, 21–23; chapter 5.2.2, printed 46–47 / PDF 78–79; Appendix B, printed 79 / PDF 111.

A **scenario** is a plausible account of future development based on coherent assumptions about driving forces and relationships. In the report's terminology, scenarios are not predictions or forecasts; they explore implications of developments and actions. The report also discusses forecasting tools, but the possibility of using such tools does not make every scenario a validated forecast.

**Resilience** is a system's ability to absorb disturbance and reorganize while retaining essential functions, structure and feedbacks. It is a neutral system property: a resilient system is not necessarily equitable or desirable. **Drought resilience** concerns the ability of communities, ecosystems and economies to anticipate, absorb, accommodate and recover from drought while preserving or improving natural capital.

**Drought** is a prolonged absence or marked deficiency of precipitation relative to normal conditions that produces hydrological imbalance and affects land resource production systems. Its impacts depend on vulnerability as well as the hazard. The report advocates proactive preparedness rather than only reacting to an unfolding crisis.

A **trade-off** occurs when an improvement in one environmental or well-being outcome entails a decline in another. A **synergy** improves more than one desirable outcome. A **co-benefit** is an additional positive effect associated with an action directed primarily at another objective; its occurrence is context-dependent and uncertain. A multifunctional landscape pursues several goals while managing these interactions, rather than assuming all benefits can be maximized simultaneously.

**Scale** includes spatial extent, spatial grain, time and analytical resolution. A watershed and a municipality may overlap but represent different decision domains. A large-area dataset can provide useful context without resolving individual land units. These distinctions affect whose impacts are counted, what can be inferred and which actors can implement a response.

## 3. Context before choosing a tool

**Source:** Chapter 3, printed 17–27 / PDF 49–59; chapter 5.3.1, printed 54–55 / PDF 86–87.

The report does not prescribe a universal planning blueprint. Political history, culture, rights and institutions create different combinations of planning arrangements. It therefore identifies nine elements rather than assigning each country to one exclusive category. A systems perspective considers their interactions, not just a checklist of isolated attributes.

| Context element                           | Question to retain when assessing a planning setting                                                                                                                                 |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **1. Governance structure**               | Where does decision-making power reside, and how much autonomy do national, regional and local actors have? Several independent centres may coordinate in a polycentric arrangement. |
| **2. State–market interactions**          | How do market decisions, public intervention and public objectives shape allocation? Economic pressures and government decisions may create continuing tensions.                     |
| **3. Land tenure**                        | Who holds formal, informal, customary, individual or communal rights? Can several rights coexist or overlap? Rights to water, trees or minerals may differ from rights to land.      |
| **4. Obligation and legitimacy of plans** | Are plans binding or discretionary, and can they be enforced? Is a locally supported plan recognized by the authorities responsible for implementation?                              |
| **5. Integration**                        | Are government tiers coordinated vertically and sectors or neighbouring areas coordinated horizontally? Fragmented decisions can undermine a landscape objective.                    |
| **6. Scope and focus**                    | Is the decision a narrow sectoral allocation or a wider reconciliation of livelihoods, conservation and environmental functions? Does it pursue multifunctionality?                  |
| **7. Actual stakeholder participation**   | Who is included, when and with what influence? Formal consultation can coexist with tokenism, unequal power or expert-only decisions.                                                |
| **8. Decision-making and planning units** | At what level are plans developed and implemented? Do boundaries follow administration, a watershed or another ecological unit? These functions may occur at different levels.       |
| **9. Policy instruments**                 | Which regulations, incentives, voluntary agreements, payments or information measures can make a plan happen? Their suitability depends on the planning context.                     |

Tenure security is important, but formal titling is neither the only way to secure rights nor an automatically effective one. Treating stakeholders as landowners alone can exclude users and people affected by a decision. Likewise, a map showing a suitable physical location does not establish permission, legitimacy or the capacity to implement a change.

The report connects tool suitability to five practical characteristics: direction of governance; decision-making scale; stakeholder involvement; accommodation of tenure types; and ability to handle biophysical and socio-economic complexity. Large-scale top-down assessments may rely more on spatial indicators and general models. Local participatory settings often need rapid appraisal and process-oriented methods alongside models. These are contextual tendencies, not rigid rules that forbid combining approaches.

## 4. The planning cycle and its feedbacks

**Source:** Chapter 4.1, printed 29–39 / PDF 61–71; Figure 4.1, printed 30 / PDF 62; Appendix A, printed 76 / PDF 108.

The report describes five generic phases: **description and assessment; visioning and goal setting; planning; implementation; monitoring and evaluation**. They are linked with the LDN modules for a frame of reference, visions and objectives, the neutrality mechanism, achieving neutrality and monitoring LDN.

The order is a useful organizing frame, not a one-way pipeline. Boundaries blur; actors may change; feedback occurs among all phases. Implementation obstacles, new participants or a natural disaster can require returning to assessment or revising goals. The result of monitoring becomes knowledge for the next decision rather than merely a final score.

### 4.1 Description and assessment

**Source:** Printed 31 / PDF 63; Box 4.1, printed 31–32 / PDF 63–64.

Understand the socio-ecological landscape and the spatial distribution of problems and beneficial functions. Identify actors, livelihoods, governance arrangements, tenure, access to ecosystem services and conflicts. Assess land condition, potential and resilience, together with socio-economic conditions and the role of gender and inclusion. These assessments support realistic location-specific goals and plausible pathways to them.

Establish a reference against which subsequent change can be interpreted. For LDN this includes baseline degradation assessment, land stratification and the other preliminary assessments needed for planning. Current condition, historical trend and restoration potential answer different questions and should not be silently substituted for one another.

Assessment can reveal opportunities but must also consider changing circumstances and governance. A location may be physically appropriate while institutionally inaccessible. Conversely, local knowledge can explain pressures or successful management practices that are not identifiable from spatial observations alone.

The Brazil example illustrates an assessment that combines land cover, water yield and sediment delivery with beneficiary objectives. It identifies candidate restoration areas before optimization. The resulting priority map is conditional on that opportunity set and the chosen objectives, not a declaration that every mapped cell is available or that the optimization itself implements restoration.

### 4.2 Visioning and goal setting

**Source:** Printed 32–34 / PDF 64–66; Boxes 4.2–4.3.

Negotiate desired landscape outcomes. Goals may be prescribed, informally developed or co-created, depending on the planning system. Different actors bring different priorities, and powerful actors—including external investors or donors—can shape what receives attention. Protecting land is one objective among several; it may complement some and compete with others.

Visioning is not a one-off activity. Goals can be revised as understanding and circumstances change. For LDN, the global vision of maintaining healthy productive land informs national ambitions for each land type. That ambition is connected to a measured reference and negotiated pathways, not simply a universal score attached to a place.

The report's participatory examples ask not only which practice is environmentally desirable, but why users would adopt it. Labour, upfront cash, time to benefit, production needs and risk can make technically recommended practices unacceptable or infeasible. Users' knowledge of a practice does not guarantee they can implement it under existing economic and institutional conditions.

### 4.3 Planning

**Source:** Printed 35–36 / PDF 67–68; Box 4.4.

Develop concrete alternatives: which interventions, in which locations and at what priority. Consider current and future demands and supplies of land resources. Analyse economic, environmental and social consequences of alternative scenarios to inform the preferred plan. Ownership and political power influence allocation even where land functions are considered common resources for present and future generations.

This phase provides the main entry point for the counterbalancing mechanism. Identify anticipated losses, prioritize prevention, consider mitigation and plan persistent gains for unavoidable losses. Like-for-like requires a defensible representation of land types and their potential. Expected long-term performance and ecosystem-service trade-offs must be considered before assuming compensation will work.

Embedding LDN in wider planning makes cumulative impacts visible. Looking only at individual restoration projects misses new degradation caused by other allocations. The report particularly values anticipating damage while alternatives still exist, rather than relying entirely on monitoring after prevention is no longer possible.

Spatial optimization can reveal conflicts among suitable uses, but the solution depends on priorities and policy constraints. The China example shows that meeting regional objectives can distribute opportunities unevenly among local administrations. Financial incentives and compensation may be necessary to implement a regionally preferred allocation.

### 4.4 Implementation

**Source:** Printed 37–39 / PDF 69–71; Box 4.5; Appendix F, printed 113–114 / PDF 145–146.

Implementation tests whether a chosen plan is viable under actual conditions. It requires spatially coordinated policies and cooperation among sectors and actors. Instruments can include regulation, incentives, support mechanisms and communication. The role of local actors matters both for compliance and for continued ownership of management.

Early participation can improve legitimacy, but appropriate institutions, finance, skills and enforcement may still be needed. A well-communicated plan does not automatically overcome customary rights, conflicting incentives, previous planning decisions or the absence of operational capacity.

Roadblocks can expose omissions in planning or arise from circumstances that were not foreseeable. The response may involve revisiting the assessment, negotiating a different intervention or changing implementation arrangements. Adaptive management is therefore part of delivery, not an admission that planning was unnecessary.

The report recommends integrating LDN into functioning planning and support systems where possible, leveraging investment and avoiding duplication. The DS-SLM example links assessment, stakeholder validation, territorial planning, demonstration, enabling policies and knowledge management. A tool is one component of that arrangement.

### 4.5 Monitoring and evaluation

**Source:** Printed 39 / PDF 71; chapter 5.3.2, printed 55–56 / PDF 87–88; Appendix A, printed 76 / PDF 108.

Monitor whether implementation is occurring, whether outcomes follow the desired trajectory and whether corrective action is needed. Reliable baseline data and suitable assessment methods are essential. Evaluation must also examine factors other than the intervention that may explain change. Observed improvement after an action is not, on its own, proof that the action caused it.

For LDN, changes in land cover, productivity and soil organic carbon are central. They do not always conclusively represent land-health status, so complementary indicators and local interpretation are recommended. Social and economic outcomes can require information beyond the three global indicators.

Actors responsible for monitoring and those evaluating results may differ. Whether regular revisions are prescribed depends on the planning system. Monitoring can trigger an intervention adjustment, a reassessment of assumptions or renewed discussion of objectives. Maintaining the learning loop matters as much as producing a final map.

## 5. Participation, rights and the limits of consensus

**Source:** Chapter 3, printed 22–25 / PDF 54–57; Box 4.2, printed 33 / PDF 65; chapter 4.2, printed 40 / PDF 72; chapters 5.2.3, 5.2.5–5.2.6 and 5.3.4, printed 48–50 and 58 / PDF 80–82 and 90.

Communities are heterogeneous. Gender, age, wealth, culture, livelihood and rights affect both preferences and influence. Participation can reproduce unequal power if these differences are ignored. A person described as a representative does not necessarily speak for every member of a group, and those most affected may never be invited to the discussion.

The report explicitly cautions against treating participatory methods as a panacea. Fair results depend on the selection of participants, facilitation and knowledge of local relationships. In some settings a combination of bottom-up and top-down processes can be more equitable than an ostensibly participatory process dominated by powerful interests. The aim is meaningful influence and a fair outcome, not participation as a label.

Important distinctions to retain are:

- **Information collection versus decision-making power.** Asking local people about conditions helps an outsider understand a place. Allowing them to define, select and negotiate actions changes their role in planning. Both can be valuable, but they serve different purposes.
- **Access versus ownership.** Users with informal or customary rights, women, young people, Indigenous communities and vulnerable ecosystem users may be affected even when they hold no formal title. Stakeholder identification must account for this.
- **Expressed consensus versus unresolved conflict.** A drive for agreement can conceal ambivalence, unpleasant choices or minority concerns. The report allows room for disagreement and recognizes that there may be winners and losers.
- **Scientific assessment versus situated understanding.** Local and traditional knowledge can contextualize observations, explain drivers and reveal important social or cultural places. Scientific indicators should inform that conversation rather than automatically dominate it.

Indigenous communities are a prominent example: their tenure, knowledge and stewardship can support conservation, while exclusion from decisions undermines both rights and implementation. Consent and partnership are important. The wider lesson is not to treat participants merely as a source of supplementary observations after objectives and options have already been fixed.

Methods must be accessible. Appendix A specifically includes alternative communication and information collection for people who are illiterate. Gender- and age-disaggregated information should be used where pertinent and feasible. These provisions are part of making knowledge and preferences visible, not simply demographic reporting requirements.

Multi-criteria analysis also raises a fairness question. A compensatory method permits weak performance on one criterion to be offset by strong performance elsewhere. Some decisions are better represented by non-compensatory methods, especially when a minority's strong preference or aversion should not disappear into an average. The report does not prescribe one universal voting or scoring rule; it asks that the method fit the decision and the participating interests.

Facilitators can structure dialogue, but restrictive survey framing, misunderstandings or poor communication can bias results. Time, trust and local knowledge are practical inputs to a participatory process. A technically sophisticated model does not remove these requirements.

## 6. Tool families and selection discipline

### 6.1 Six groups serve different questions

**Source:** Figure 5.1 and Table 5.1, printed 42–44 / PDF 74–76; chapters 5.2.1–5.2.7, printed 45–51 / PDF 77–83.

The report groups tools along two broad dimensions: the role of stakeholder participation versus expert analysis, and the representation of current conditions versus future dynamics. Categories overlap. A platform may combine several methods; a familiar interface does not determine the underlying analytical function.

| Group                       | Main question and function                                                                                                                                                  | Main phases                                                | Limit to remember                                                                                                                              |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Indicator assessment**    | What is the current condition or historical trend? Derive and interpret biophysical or socio-economic indicators from measurements, spatial data, statistics or surveys.    | Assessment; monitoring                                     | A mapped indicator is not automatically an explanation of cause or a model of future response.                                                 |
| **Forward-looking tools**   | What might happen under specified drivers, interventions and constraints? Explore future land patterns or socio-economic and biophysical outcomes.                          | Visioning; planning; reference trajectories for monitoring | Predictions depend on assumptions and input quality; simple extrapolation can fail under changing climate and other non-stationary conditions. |
| **Multi-criteria analysis** | How do defined alternatives perform against competing objectives and different stakeholder preferences? Structure, compare and rank options.                                | Visioning; planning; implementation                        | Weights and aggregation embody values; representation and compensatory assumptions affect fairness.                                            |
| **Optimization**            | Which allocation best meets objectives and constraints across the landscape when alternatives are numerous or not pre-enumerated?                                           | Planning; visioning                                        | An optimum is relative to its formulation. Inputs, objectives and constraints do not become correct because the algorithm solves them.         |
| **Rapid appraisal**         | What do local people know about conditions, uses, rights and past interventions? Collect qualitative and contextual information through interviews, mapping or observation. | Assessment; monitoring; inputs to other phases             | Framing and facilitation can bias findings; local observations do not directly produce all quantitative LDN indicators.                        |
| **Process-oriented tools**  | How can actors negotiate feasible actions, responsibilities, preferences and compensation? Support local decision-making and ownership.                                     | Planning; implementation; knowledge exchange               | Success depends on participants, accepted facilitation, power relationships and connection to implementation.                                  |

Indicator tools can combine land-condition trends with environmental and social information to investigate pressures and drivers. Such relationships may be assessed statistically or through expert and local judgment. Field observations and rapid appraisal can validate or contextualize a broad spatial assessment; neither dataset resolution nor a statistical relationship should be treated as a substitute for that context.

Forward-looking tools can be spatially explicit land-use models, economic models, integrated models or other forms such as urban-growth and agent-based models. Some estimate commodity demand while others allocate that demand to locations. Combining them can be useful, provided their scales and assumptions are compatible. Future scenarios can be co-developed with stakeholders so that livelihoods, cultural conditions and local objectives are represented.

Optimization differs from ranking a fixed list of alternatives. It searches a potentially very large space of allocations under defined objectives. Interactive approaches can involve stakeholders during the search. Where several objectives conflict, a single preferred solution should not be mistaken for the only legitimate future.

### 6.2 Constraints and combinations

**Source:** Box 5.2, printed 53 / PDF 85; chapter 5.3, printed 54–59 / PDF 86–91.

Data availability, resolution, computational facilities, expertise, cost and human capacity constrain application. A detailed model may be unsuitable when required inputs are unavailable. A coarse model can reveal regional patterns while missing local heterogeneity. Greater complexity increases technical and interpretation demands; it does not automatically improve usefulness.

Combining tools can address limitations, but introduces interoperability problems. Shared file formats do not guarantee matching definitions, classifications or spatial resolution. Harmonizing inputs can introduce uncertainty and inaccuracy. Planning therefore requires attention to what one tool's output means when it becomes another's input.

The report's selection matrices distinguish scale, governance direction, complexity, participation and tenure. Their general logic is:

1. Use indicator assessment for broad-area baseline and monitoring questions; combine it with local appraisal when local interpretation or validation is needed.
2. Use forward-looking and optimization methods to explore allocations and consequences; combine them with participatory scenario analysis or preference analysis where stakeholders shape the alternatives.
3. Use process-oriented methods for negotiating action; add multi-criteria approaches where conflicting objectives need explicit comparison and tenure-sensitive methods where rights differ.

These are selection principles, not a catalogue of mandatory software. The report's closing argument is that tools are not solutions in themselves. Institutions and people choose and apply them within social, political, economic and ecological contexts. Capacity to understand and use their results, and links to actual planning systems, determine implementation quality.

### 6.3 Future change, causality and modelling limits

**Source:** Chapters 4.1 and 5.2.2, printed 39 and 46–47 / PDF 71 and 78–79; Box 5.3, printed 57 / PDF 89; Appendix C, printed 81–82 / PDF 113–114.

Spatial land-use models commonly combine demands for land-based commodities, supply associated with land systems, local suitability and conversion constraints. Suitability can depend on biophysical and socio-economic conditions; rules can represent policy restrictions or neighbourhood effects. These components explain why changing one use may displace demand or alter opportunities elsewhere.

The report describes cascading land-use change explicitly: some uses respond directly to human demand, while others change because the first allocation has occurred. A scenario can therefore propagate consequences through a landscape rather than evaluate every parcel independently. Which relationships are represented depends on the model, and a report example does not establish a universal quantitative coefficient.

Under climate change and other non-stationary conditions, linear continuation of historical trajectories may be unsuitable. Scenarios should expose assumptions about drivers and responses. Models can identify potential conflicts, timelines or reference trajectories, but projected values retain the limitations of those assumptions and data.

Implementing neutrality remains demanding. The report says no land-use model had implemented the counterbalancing mechanism in its full intended extent at the time reviewed. Examples approximate indicators with averages for land systems or use biogeographical regions as land types. These compromises can omit local terrain, soils and ecological differences. They must remain visible when interpreting an apparently precise result.

## 7. Selected tools and approaches

The following cards consolidate relevant entries from chapters 2–5 and Appendices C–E. They retain purpose and limitations rather than reproduce a complete software directory. Their selection is editorial; their descriptions are historical accounts from the report.

### 7.1 Serious Gaming

**Source:** Appendix E, printed 104 / PDF 136. **OCR-restored:** the supplied Markdown retained only the header/footer; the complete source-table entry was recovered from raw text and visually checked against the original PDF.

Serious Gaming includes simulation gaming, game-based modelling, games with a purpose and role games. It embeds decision problems in a gaming experience. Example decisions include allocating resources, choosing a land-use type and setting prices. It supports scenario development, understanding household decision-making, stakeholder engagement and education.

Its stated relevance to LDN includes examining the socio-economic environment, facilitating stakeholder negotiations and supporting knowledge exchange. The report cites applications in Germany, Ghana, Laos and Senegal. Those references establish example applications; they do not prescribe their rules, audiences or parameters for every setting.

The main reported challenge is preparation: studying the region, understanding which options belong in the game, creating an engaging and challenging experience, and defining a clear goal. The entry does not frame a game as a substitute for regional knowledge or present scientific accuracy as guaranteed by an interactive interface.

### 7.2 Participatory scenario analysis and mapping

**Source:** Appendix C, printed 84–85 / PDF 116–117; Appendix E, printed 103–104 / PDF 135–136. Mapping content on PDF 136 was restored alongside Serious Gaming.

Participatory scenario analysis compares potential futures, including best- and worst-case scenarios, in a group setting. Participants identify present conditions, driving forces, implications and common ground. The resulting scenarios can become inputs to forward-looking models. Smart-mapping represents alternative futures through computer images that can be updated over time.

Participatory mapping allows individuals or groups to draw a geographic unit with important boundaries, resources and places, including tenure holdings and areas of conflict. Additional information can be attached through notes or physical objects. Maps are compared and revised through discussion. Community mapping may begin from aerial or land-cover imagery and then incorporate local knowledge of important sites.

Both methods require local knowledge and a trained, accepted facilitator. Mapping a preference is different from establishing a legal boundary. The report presents these methods as ways to reveal conditions, negotiate and exchange knowledge, not as automatic resolution of contested claims.

### 7.3 LUP4LDN

**Source:** Box 5.1, printed 52 / PDF 84; Appendix E, printed 97 / PDF 129.

The report describes Land Use Planning for Land Degradation Neutrality as the winning proposal from a 2020 GEO-LDN technology innovation competition. Led by SCiO with domain experts, it was designed to support transparent, participatory planning through a user-friendly interface, collaborative transition scenarios, charts and maps showing impacts and trade-offs.

Users can examine past and current degradation, choose intervention areas, select sustainable land-management innovations and compare scenarios. Stakeholder suitability evaluations and expert assessments inform anticipated degradation. The tool translates user scenarios into LDN scenarios, representing impacts on the three global indicators. It connects with the WOCAT framework and database and established tools such as Trends.Earth and GeOC.

The report identifies policymakers, advisors, planning organizations, land users and researchers as target groups. It describes both workshop use and remote collaboration. Appendix E notes that the tool was still in development. This is a direct precedent for collaborative scenario exploration, but the report does not independently demonstrate its present availability, accuracy or deployment outcomes.

### 7.4 Trends.Earth and local validation

**Source:** Chapter 5.2.1, printed 45–46 / PDF 77–78; Appendix C, printed 80 / PDF 112; Appendix E, printed 92 / PDF 124.

Trends.Earth combines spatial datasets and algorithms in a consistent workflow for assessing degradation and monitoring change. The report describes default global data for the three indicators, with custom regional inputs and adjustments to the land-cover conversion matrix. Local knowledge can inform those adjustments and interpretation.

The review notes limits: additional indicators were not then supported beyond the three, spatial skills were needed, and local applications benefited from higher-resolution regional data. These are capabilities as reviewed in the report, not a current feature audit. Visual soil or vegetation guides, site visits and local assessment can complement remote sensing, although visual assessment is not the same as a measured quantitative indicator.

### 7.5 CLUMondo and LANDSCAPE

**Source:** Box 5.3, printed 57 / PDF 89; Appendix C, printed 81–82 / PDF 113–114; Appendix E, printed 95 / PDF 127. The Appendix E page was recovered from raw text and cross-checked with the original PDF.

CLUMondo allocates future land-system patterns from demand, supply, suitability and conversion rules. Its LDN application adds demand for no net loss and uses a conversion matrix to identify loss, gain or stability. Average indicator values are assigned to land systems and compared according to one-out, all-out. Like-for-like is partly represented through biogeographical regions.

The report warns that averaging can ignore local terrain or soil properties and that the reviewed implementation restricted the number of regions for counterbalancing. It is an example of explicitly integrating neutrality into land allocation, with acknowledged approximations rather than complete implementation of the scientific framework.

LANDSCAPE uses hierarchical allocation and land-use resistance. It distinguishes active changes directly associated with demand from passive changes induced by them. Different uses have different allocation priority or aggressiveness. Its relevance here is the representation of cascading change: one allocation initiates another. The report describes applications to land-use strategies and cropland protection, while identifying spatial inputs, expert assumptions and access limitations.

### 7.6 ROOT and restoration opportunities

**Source:** Box 4.1, printed 31–32 / PDF 63–64; Appendix C, printed 84 / PDF 116; Appendix E, printed 100 / PDF 132. The latter table was absent from the Markdown body and recovered from the original source.

The Restoration Opportunities Optimization Tool identifies locations for restoration to meet multiple ecosystem-service objectives, beneficiary needs and land-use constraints. The report describes combining spatial demands and expected biophysical changes, running optimizations with changing objective weights and synthesizing them into agreement maps.

Agreement indicates how often a location is selected under the modelled choices; it is not a probability of ecological success or evidence that affected people consent. Spatial data, technical facilities and local knowledge are required. Appendix E distinguishes existing optimization capabilities from possible adaptations for LDN, such as incorporating indicator maps or like-for-like regions. Those adaptations must not be reported as already implemented.

### 7.7 Multi-criteria approaches and OPTamos

**Source:** Chapter 5.2.3, printed 48 / PDF 80; Box 3.3, printed 28 / PDF 60; Appendix C, printed 82–83 / PDF 114–115; Appendix E, printed 98 / PDF 130.

Analytical Hierarchy Process uses pairwise comparisons to structure criteria and preferences. Multi-Attribute Utility Theory combines weighted, normalized criteria values; the assigned weights are partly subjective. Outranking approaches compare alternatives using preferences, indifference and concordance/discordance. They can preserve strong concerns that a simple compensatory total might obscure.

OPTamos supports social multi-criteria evaluation with stakeholder-defined goals, options and criteria. Its report entry describes pairwise comparison, consistency checking and sensitivity analysis. The Mexico case links it to workshops rather than treating scoring as a replacement for deliberation. The practical distinction is between modelled performance on a criterion and the human judgment of how important that criterion is.

### 7.8 Local knowledge, rights and decision-support processes

**Source:** Box 2.1, printed 14–15 / PDF 46–47; Box 4.3, printed 33–34 / PDF 65–66; Appendices C and E, printed 80–85 and 92–104 / PDF 112–117 and 124–136.

The WOCAT-LADA-DESIRE approach combines spatial mapping with specialist assessment of drivers. LADA-Local combines biophysical assessment, transect walks, livelihood interviews and group discussion to negotiate responses. ELMO examines users' motivations, resource requirements and perceived benefits and disadvantages of management practices.

The Social Tenure Domain Model addresses formal and informal tenure together, using spatial information and on-the-ground surveys. LandPKS combines geospatial information with local observations and crowdsourcing. The RDDST dashboard sits within SHARED's wider process of contextualizing, integrating evidence, prioritizing and planning, and learning and responding.

These entries preserve several complementary functions: characterize rights and actors; validate condition; understand adoption; negotiate options; and connect evidence with action. They should not be collapsed into the claim that a single observation platform or dashboard performs the entire planning process.

On printed page 104, the report also describes **triangulation of participatory methods**: collect feedback on an initial method and adapt it to improve engagement, team-building and ownership. **Green Negotiated Territorial Development (GreeNTD)** emphasizes strengthening weaker stakeholders and balancing competing demands through an inclusive negotiation process. Both add time and preparation; their contribution is procedural, not a new biophysical equation.

## 8. Cases and environmental themes worth retaining

### 8.1 Six short case cards

These are accounts presented by the report. Specific outcomes are not independently evaluated here, and local parameters should not be transferred to a different region without justification.

**Turkana County, Kenya — evidence and an iterative shared process.** The SHARED framework supported a more integrated county development plan with an RDDST dashboard combining social, economic and biophysical information. Its four steps were contextualize; integrate evidence; prioritize and plan; learn and respond. The important relationship is between a shared decision process and an evidence tool, not a dashboard operating alone. The report describes more inclusive cross-sector initiatives and greater capacity for evidence-based decisions. **Source:** Box 2.1, printed 14–15 / PDF 46–47.

**Northern watersheds, Honduras — scenarios co-developed with stakeholders.** A landscape partnership brought together producers, Indigenous people, communities, municipalities and researchers. Three scenarios—business as usual, accelerated agricultural export growth and an integrated landscape—were explored with land-use, ecosystem-service and biodiversity models. Stakeholders' ambitions informed the scenarios; models then exposed consequences and potential conflicts. The example preserves the sequence of local priorities, scenario assumptions, spatial rules and multiple outcomes. It does not imply that all three modelling systems are necessary in every project. **Source:** Box 3.1, printed 24–25 / PDF 56–57.

**Espírito Santo, Brazil — restoration targeted to functions and beneficiaries.** Drought, heat and degradation motivated action on water and sediment retention. Candidate restoration areas were assessed, then ROOT identified locations offering multiple benefits while considering low-income populations in priority watersheds. Agreement maps and clusters helped inform a payment-for-ecosystem-services programme. The lesson is to connect location, expected function, beneficiaries and implementation incentives. Exact hectares and monetary benefits are omitted because they do not establish transferable parameters. **Source:** Box 4.1, printed 31–32 / PDF 63–64.

**Cuitzmala watershed, Mexico — criteria and alternatives emerge through dialogue.** Stakeholders identified issues, perceived causal links, a desired future, criteria and possible interventions. OPTamos supported comparison of conservation with payments, mixed use and industrial agriculture. Mixed use attracted the broadest support because participants considered environmental functions alongside equity, corruption and cultural identity. Transparency and structured discussion helped motivate ownership of actions. The case demonstrates why the preferred option cannot always be inferred from a single environmental metric. **Source:** Box 3.3, printed 28 / PDF 60.

**Tanzania and Malawi — adoption requires understanding constraints.** The AGORA project and ELMO examined preferences among practices such as grass strips, trees, terraces and composting. Discussions considered resource requirements, benefits, risks, labour, cash and time to returns. Women and men across age and wealth groups were included. The lesson is that people may understand sustainable practices yet be unable or unwilling to adopt a technically favoured option under their actual constraints. The report also records a stakeholder forum continuing after the project. **Source:** Box 4.3, printed 33–34 / PDF 65–66.

**Western Cape, South Africa — ecological functions extend beyond protected boundaries.** Spatial biodiversity planning identified critical areas and ecological support areas, including restoration priorities. The aim was to integrate biodiversity into wider development and planning instruments, using representation and long-term persistence, including corridors. Protected areas alone were insufficient. The example retains the need for spatial connection and compatibility across planning scales; the provincial classification and exact area statistics are not reproduced. **Source:** Box 3.2, printed 26–27 / PDF 58–59.

Two further examples are retained only as boundary lessons. **Poyang Lake, China** illustrates allocation under policy quotas and the uneven distribution of conservation and development opportunities; implementation may require compensation. **The Three-North Shelterbelt Program** illustrates long-term coordination, different forest functions and diversified financing. Neither example establishes that a particular planting programme or allocation rule transfers unchanged to another setting. **Source:** Boxes 4.4–4.5, printed 35–39 / PDF 67–71.

### 8.2 Connected environmental and livelihood pressures

**Source:** Chapter 2, printed 10–13 / PDF 42–45; Appendix B, printed 77–79 / PDF 109–111; Appendix G, printed 115 / PDF 147.

**Food, energy and nature compete for finite land.** An action beneficial for one goal can displace pressure onto another use or location. The report gives the example of bioenergy production replacing food crops and indirectly encouraging land clearance elsewhere. A landscape perspective considers these dependencies, including opportunities such as residues or degraded-land restoration, instead of treating each intervention as an isolated benefit.

**Urban and rural systems are linked.** Livelihoods, migration, food supply and ecological functions cross administrative and urban–rural boundaries. Peri-urban areas can face both development pressure and opportunities for restoration, parks, revegetation or agriculture. Preserving a local function while displacing its pressure elsewhere is not necessarily a net improvement at landscape scale.

**Drought preparedness spans several sectors.** The report retains three pillars: monitoring and early warning; vulnerability and risk assessment; and mitigation measures. Examples include water harvesting, restoring pastures, soil-water retention, irrigation improvements, livelihood diversification and drought-tolerant crops. Their value depends on coordination, suitability and local conditions. The list is a menu of possible responses, not evidence that every action has the same effect everywhere.

**Other themes remain part of the wider context.** Land-use change can affect disease exposure and sand/dust sources. The detailed pandemic-era discussion and dust statistics are omitted here, but the cross-sector principle is preserved: an action can affect environmental, health and economic outcomes together.

The Jadar mining example makes a related point: a project contributing to energy-transition demand can conflict with local farming, rights, health concerns, biodiversity and LDN commitments. The report's historical project quantities and projections are not reproduced or represented as current facts. The retained lesson is the need to evaluate competing benefits and burdens rather than equate an international objective with unqualified local sustainability.

## 9. Appendix A: retained LDN criteria checklist

**Source:** Appendix A, printed 76 / PDF 108. The original table was visually checked. This is a lightly normalized restatement retaining its criteria, grouped by planning phase. These are the report's criteria for LDN implementation, not a list of mandatory features for every game or local prototype.

### Assessment — Module B: frame of reference

- Consider at least the three global indicators—NPP, soil organic carbon and land cover—for assessment, potentially alongside national or regional indicators.
- Average indicator values over the 10–15 years preceding the baseline year.
- Validate the assessment with local knowledge.

### Visioning — Module A: visions and objectives

Recognize ecological functions and increase resilience:

- Determine biophysical and socio-economic drivers of degradation.
- Identify natural and anthropogenic pressures.
- Analyse restoration potential.

Support responsible and inclusive governance:

- Identify and account for land tenure rights.
- Consider different holding and tenure types, including small and large holders, without giving one automatic preference.
- Use gender- and age-disaggregated data in analysis.
- Consult representatives across gender, age and Indigenous groups, accounting for dynamic tenure arrangements, informal governance and statutory and customary laws.

### Planning — Module C: mechanism for neutrality

- Count anticipated areas of future loss and proposed areas of restoration.
- Represent no net loss in the analysis.
- Base the no-net-loss simulation on at least the three global indicators.
- Apply like-for-like counterbalancing based on land potential.

### Implementation — Module D: achieving neutrality

- Identify and characterize stakeholders.
- Use multi-stakeholder platforms to connect scientific assessment with local knowledge and coordinate across sectors.
- Include different economic, political and cultural groups; meaningful participation of women and men; and representation of young and Indigenous people.
- Use alternative communication and information-collection methods to include illiterate people.
- Facilitate knowledge sharing between technical experts and land users.
- Ensure local ownership of results.

### Monitoring — Module E: monitoring LDN

- Monitor at least the three global indicators.
- Integrate results through one-out, all-out.
- Validate and interpret monitoring results with local knowledge.
- Allow false positives to be reported.

## 10. Policy direction, selection and provenance

### 10.1 Policy proposals in brief

**Source:** Policy Proposals, printed xxvi–xxviii / PDF 28–30; chapter 5.3.5, printed 59 / PDF 91.

The report recommends strengthening cross-sector governance and planning capacity; collaboration between researchers and practitioners to develop or adapt tools; and knowledge generation and exchange across institutions, communities and open-source repositories. It advocates integrating LDN target-setting into national and subnational planning rather than operating a disconnected parallel process.

It also calls for coordination with biodiversity, climate and sustainable-development goals. Investments should support not just technical tools, but institutions, skills, documentation and learning. Planning projects should record methods, outcomes, benefits, challenges and shortcomings. These proposals preserve the connection between a tool and its sustained use without reproducing detailed national funding and administrative recommendations.
